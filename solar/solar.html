Planet orbits — to scale

A 3D view of all eight planets' orbits — it opens top-down, flat on Earth's orbit (the view the Reset view button always returns to), and you can drag with a finger or mouse to turn it and tilt it to nearly edge-on. The zoom slider runs from this view, Earth's orbit filling the card, out to the whole system, stopping where Neptune's orbit — about 60 AU across — is just fully in view. The orbits themselves are fixed; they don't follow the clock or Play button above. Every ring keeps its true scale, shape and tilt at every zoom. Earth's ellipse has eccentricity 0.0167, its minor axis 99.986% of its major axis, so at true scale it is almost indistinguishable from a circle; the real effect is the Sun sitting off-centre at a focus, 0.0167 AU (about 2.5 million km) from the orbit's centre (marked with a cross at the default zoom). The rings tilt against Earth's plane — Mercury's by 7.0°, Venus's 3.4°, Mars's 1.85°, Saturn's 2.49°, Jupiter's 1.30°, Neptune's 1.77° and Uranus's 0.77° — each ring tilted against Earth's orbital plane, crossing above and below it. The Sun is drawn at its true size at the default zoom — diameter 1.393 million km = 0.0093 AU, under half a percent of Earth's orbit's width. Zoomed out, its true size would shrink to about a tenth of a pixel, so there it is drawn enlarged, just big enough to stay visible; the orbits remain to scale at every zoom. Earth itself would be a few hundredths of a pixel across at this scale, which is why the views above exaggerate body sizes. The dots mark Earth's actual position on the 1st of each month of the displayed year (January's is an amber diamond, so the start of the year stays easy to spot while you turn the view) — spaced slightly wider near perihelion in early January, where Earth moves fastest, and tighter near aphelion in early July; the labels on the orbit itself give that year's exact perihelion and aphelion dates and distances. At this scale the Moon's orbit would be a circle just 768,800 km (0.00514 AU) across, with a radius of 384,400 km (0.00257 AU) — 0.257% of Earth's orbit's width and only about 55% of the Sun's diameter, roughly 1–2 px across on desktop and under 1 px on a phone, smaller than the Sun dot drawn here; the Moon itself would be about 0.007 px across.

Why we have seasons

Earth's axis is tilted 23.44° and — the key point — it keeps pointing at the same spot in space (near the star Polaris) all year. In the top view, watch the axis marker on Earth: it never swivels to follow the Sun. In June the Northern Hemisphere is tipped toward the Sun, so sunlight arrives more directly and days are longer there; in December it is tipped away. The seasons come from that fixed tilt, not from Earth's distance — Earth is actually closest to the Sun (perihelion) in early January, in the middle of northern winter.

Why the Moon has phases

The Moon shines by reflected sunlight, so exactly half of it is always lit — the half facing the Sun. As the Moon circles Earth every 27.32 days, we see that lit half from different angles: at new Moon the lit half faces away from us, at full Moon it faces us completely, and at the quarters we see it half-and-half. The disc in the upper right of the Face-on view shows the phase exactly as it appears from Earth at the displayed moment. A full cycle of phases (the synodic month) takes 29.53 days — a little longer than the orbit itself, because Earth has moved along its own orbit in the meantime.

Why there isn't an eclipse every month

The Moon's orbit is tilted 5.14° to the ecliptic (Earth's orbital plane), so at most new and full Moons the Moon passes above or below the Sun–Earth line and no shadow lines up. Eclipses can only occur when a new or full Moon happens while the Moon is also near a node — one of the two points where its orbit crosses the ecliptic. Those node crossings drift backwards (regress) through the calendar over an 18.6-year cycle, which is why eclipse seasons move. The readout flags these windows when the Moon is near a node at new or full Moon.
